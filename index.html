HOOK

Language for Elevator Pitch

Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

  • engage the audience by asking personalised questions
  • immerse the audience in a vivid scenario using scene starters
  • use appropriate language to hook your listener at the start of an elevator pitch

Apart from the four techniques introduced in Unit 2 (surprising facts/statistics, compelling questions, vivid stories, and citing quotations), you can engage the audience by asking personalised questions to connect to their experiences or challenges.

Examples:

"Are you tired of rough prototypes failing to capture the essence of your design?"
"Have you ever wondered why some exhibition displays captivate visitors in seconds?"
"How many of you have experienced screen-to-print colour mismatches ruining your final pieces?"
"Can you recall a time when hidden bugs crashed your game prototype during a playtest?"
"Are you tired of joining video calls that freeze at the most critical moments?"
"Have you ever wondered what prevents the entire Internet from collapsing?"
"How many of you have experienced irritating bugs in newly developed games?"
"Can you recall a time when a website failed you during an urgent transaction?"

Use scene starters to vividly immerse the audience in a scenario and spark their imagination.

Examples:

"Think about a performer with sensory issues putting on a sequinned costume that scratches all night."
"Imagine spending weeks on a costume collection, only to find that the fabric cannot withstand the performers' dynamic stage movements."
"Envision yourself unable to meet client expectations due to gaps between concept and execution."
"Picture yourself trying to protect your organisation's systems from ever-evolving cyber threats."
"Remember excitedly downloading a new app, only to have it crash the moment you opened it?"

Work in pairs. Choose a personalised question or scene starter above to hook your partner. Then switch roles.

You have 5 minutes to practise.

PROBLEM

Relating to the Audience

Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

  • use useful verbs and nouns to describe problems
  • use present participle clauses to emphasise negative consequences
  • relate to the audience by acknowledging shared struggles

Sentence Starters:

"Many of us have experienced ..."
"Time and again, we find ourselves ..."
"We've all faced moments when ..."
"It's a familiar situation when ..."

These clauses emphasise the negative consequences of a problem.

Problem: "...designers are stuck in endless email chains, painful revisions, and missed deadlines,"

Consequence: leading to / resulting in / bringing about / giving rise to wasted time, money, and creativity.

Problem: "...many companies still operate with outdated cybersecurity systems,"

Consequence: creating a greater risk of cyber attacks that could disrupt operations.

Problem: "...large language models (LLMs) may produce inappropriate or offensive responses,"

Consequence: generating safety concerns for users and platforms.

Create your own problem + consequence pair using a present participle clause. Share with your partner.

Pattern: [Problem], leading to / resulting in / creating [Consequence].

CA Sample Script — DataInsight Elevator Pitch More CA Samples Download Sample Scripts

Overview

This is a sample elevator pitch script for the CA assessment. It demonstrates how to structure a pitch using the Hook, Problem, Solution, UVP, and Call to Action sections you have learned.

Self-introduction

"Hello, everyone. I'm Tony from Synix. At Synix, our mission is to empower businesses to transform data into actionable insights for informed decision-making."

Hook

"Have you, like many other business owners, struggled to extract meaningful insights from vast amounts of data?"

Problem statement

"The frustration of seeing valuable data sit idle knowing it could unlock business opportunities and drive success—it's an obstacle we know all too well."

Description of pain points using emotional appeal

"Many of us find it challenging to tap into the potential of underutilised data. Without effective analysis tools, we may overlook key patterns and trends, leading to missed opportunities, inefficient decisions, and lost competitive advantages."

Solution

"To address these challenges, I'm excited to introduce DataInsight, AI-powered software that makes data transformation effortless. Its real-time analytics enable instant data processing, while its predictive modelling helps you forecast trends and make informed decisions."

Unique value proposition (UVP)

"What makes DataInsight unique is its advanced AI-driven analysis. By rapidly processing complex datasets, it delivers accurate predictions, empowering your business with a competitive edge through data-driven decisions."

UVP — Why it is valuable to the audience

"With DataInsight, I'm confident that you will uncover hidden insights and achieve impactful outcomes."

Call to action

"Ready to turn your data into a powerful asset? Adopt DataInsight now and drive business growth."

Closing

"Thank you."

INTONATION PRACTICE

Intonation for Pitch Video

In acknowledgement of Christine Tsang

1. What is intonation?

Intonation = how your voice goes up and down when you speak. It shows attitude, emotion and meaning, not just words. Good intonation makes your pitch sound confident, engaging and easy to follow.

We will focus on three patterns:

  • Falling intonation (↘): voice goes down at the end – common in statements and wh‑ questions.
  • Rising intonation (↗): voice goes up at the end – common in yes/no questions, showing doubt or surprise.
  • Non‑final intonation (↗ … ↘): small rise in the middle of a long sentence, then a fall at the end to show you are continuing.

A. Humming exercise

Close your eyes and hum "mmmm" at a comfortable pitch for 5 seconds.

Now say "I'm a designer" but keep the same relaxed feeling in your throat and face.

Repeat with: "I'm an IT student." "I'm ready to pitch my idea."

Goal: Notice how your pitch naturally moves up and down when you say a normal sentence.

Listen to the examples:

"I'm a designer."

"I'm an IT student."

"I'm ready to pitch my idea."

B. Flat vs expressive

Your teacher (or a classmate) reads each sentence twice: completely flat, then with natural intonation.

Sentences:

"I'd like to show you my design."

"Today I'm going to talk about my app."

"Our product solves a big problem."

Tick the version that sounds more interesting and professional.

Check your answers with your teacher, then say each line 3 times together (chorus), focusing on the final pitch movement.

Practise each sentence twice:

Example: "You finished the project."

a) ↘ (statement, sure)

b) ↗ (question, surprise / doubt)

Now try:

"You fixed the bug."

"You designed this yourself."

"You want more feedback."

In pairs, take turns: Student A says version (a) or (b), Student B guesses "statement" or "question/uncertain".

In a pitch, some words are more important. We stress them by making the vowel longer, louder, and often higher in pitch.

A. Find the key words

Underline the most important content words in each sentence. Then listen to your teacher and repeat.

"Today I'll present our new task‑management app for students."

"Our goal is to make project deadlines easier to manage."

"This design focuses on a simple, clean interface."

"We tested it with 20 users and got very positive feedback."

Suggested answers (for teacher): content words like "present / new / app / students", "goal / project / deadlines", etc.

B. Stress shift and meaning

Say this sentence with stress on different words. Mark the main stress with CAPITALS and ↘ at the end.

Sentence: "We designed this app for busy students.↘"

WE designed this app for busy students.↘ (not another team)

We DESIGNED this app for busy students.↘ (not just talked about it)

We designed THIS app for busy students.↘ (not another app)

We designed this APP for busy students.↘ (not a website)

In pairs, choose two versions and act them with body language. Partner guesses which word you stressed most and why.

B. Pitch lines

Practise these lines with different feelings (teacher models one option):

"This feature saves users a lot of time."

In longer sentences, we often use a small rise (↗) in the middle and a fall (↘) at the end to show the sentence is not finished yet.

Example: "First, I'll describe the problem↗ then I'll show you our solution.↘"

"First, I'll describe the problem↗ then I'll show you our solution.↘"

A. Mark thought groups

Add slashes (/) for pauses and mark ↗ or ↘.

"First I'll explain the problem then I'll show you our solution."

"Our app helps users track tasks and it also reminds them about deadlines."

"We interviewed real users and we improved the design based on their feedback."

Possible marking (for teacher):

  • "First I'll explain the problem↗ / then I'll show you our solution.↘"
  • "Our app helps users track tasks↗ / and it also reminds them about deadlines.↘"

Students practise in pairs, using clear pauses and pitch changes.

Shadowing = listening to a model and speaking at the same time, copying rhythm, stress, and intonation as closely as possible.

A. Teacher model shadowing

Teacher reads each sentence twice with strong, clear intonation. Students:

  • Listen only.
  • Shadow (speak together).
  • Speak alone without the model.

Sentences (adapt for your project):

"Today I'm going to present our smart study planner app.↘"

"It helps students organise tasks, set priorities, and never miss a deadline.↘"

"First, I'll show you the problem↗ then I'll show you how our design solves it.↘"

"At the end, I'd love to hear your feedback and questions.↘"

Students circle the sentence they can say most confidently and use it later in their own pitch.

A. Prepare (5–7 minutes)

Individually, write 3–4 sentences you could use in your real pitch video, for example:

  • Opening line
  • One sentence describing the problem
  • One sentence describing the solution or key feature
  • Closing line

Underline key words and add arrows ↗ / ↘ at the end of each sentence.

Examples:

"Today I'll present our health‑tracking app for young athletes.↘"

"It helps users track their performance and avoid injuries.↘"

B. Practise in pairs (10–15 minutes)

Student A gives their mini‑pitch (about 30–40 seconds).

Student B listens and fills in this checklist:

  • Key words are stressed clearly.
  • Sentences end with clear ↘ or ↗.
  • Voice is not flat; emotion/attitude is clear.

Student B gives one praise and one suggestion about intonation only.

Switch roles.

A. First recording (individual, homework or class if time)

Record a 45–90 second version of your pitch using your phone.

  • Listen once for content.
  • Listen again, now focus only on intonation: underline any part that sounds flat or uncertain.

B. Edit and upgrade

  • Choose 2–3 sentences that sound too flat.
  • On your paper, add arrows, underline key words, and decide the emotion you want to show (confident, enthusiastic, serious, etc.).
  • Practise those sentences 3–4 times each with stronger intonation.

C. Second recording

Record the same pitch again with improved intonation. Answer these reflection questions:

  • Which version sounds more engaging? Why?
  • Which sentence shows your best intonation? Write it here and mark the stress and arrows.

You can submit both versions to your teacher (or upload to your LMS) to show your progress.

Before you submit your pitch video, check:

  • My key message words are clear and stressed.
  • Most of my statements end with a confident ↘.
  • My questions sound like questions (↗ when needed).
  • Long sentences have a small rise in the middle ↗ and a fall ↘ at the end.
  • My voice shows a suitable attitude (interested, confident, professional), not flat or bored.
